## 10. Tensions in the portrait

The marvellous side of the portrait grows with lateness. The table gives the share of texts in each group in which gods, rebirth or past lives, and wonders were flagged.

| Group | Texts | Gods | Rebirth or past lives | Wonders |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Four main Nikāyas | 3,413 | 25% | 29% | 9% |
| Other early Khuddaka | 691 | 31% | 46% | 15% |
| Jātaka | 547 | 58% | 97% | 41% |
| Late Khuddaka | 998 | 44% | 78% | 28% |
| Vinaya | 422 | 12% | 9% | 8% |
| Abhidhamma | 402 | 20% | 40% | 15% |
| Dhammapada stories | 308 | 45% | 60% | 46% |

Wonders rise from 9% of Nikāya texts to over 40% of the Jātaka and the Dhammapada stories; rebirth or past lives from 29% to 97% of the Jātaka. The Vinaya is the most this-worldly layer. A flag marks a mention, not a central theme.

### 10.1 Cosmology and rebirth beside the empirical stance [Strong]

*Evidence: 1,063 texts in 35 collections, 527 of them early; 1,226 lines support, 43 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 414, early Khuddaka 113, Jātaka 149, late Khuddaka 146, Vinaya 36, Abhidhamma 93, Dhammapada stories 112.*

The texts present devas, heavens, hells, and rebirth as matters he knows directly by trained perception (MN 36:38.1–40.2; AN 8.64; DN 18). AN 8.64 shows the tension inside one text: a careful, staged, empirical procedure applied to visions of gods. Every book in the series found rebirth contradicted by neuroscience and the cosmology contradicted by astronomy. AN 4.45:6.3 ("in this fathom-long carcass... I describe the world") supports a psychological reading but does not erase the literal one.

This is the most attested trait in the profile, and rebirth is structural, not decorative: "the craving that leads to future lives" (DN 22:19.2), consciousness descending into the womb (DN 15:21.2), the stages of awakening defined by rebirth (DN 6:13.2). He names his past selves (DN 5:21.16), declares the rebirths of the dead (DN 18:1.4), and tells monks to "learn the Āṭānāṭiya protection!" (DN 32:13.1–13.4).

The complicating lines, nearly all early, keep this world at a distance:
- **Heaven real but no goal:** practising for divine rebirth is a fetter of the heart (MN 16:12.1; AN 9.72:2.4).
- **Gods demoted:** a verse "poorly sung by Sakka" (AN 3.37:6.1); no god can stop ageing or kamma (AN 4.182:1.2).
- **Māra read psychologically:** where there is no mind, "there is no Māra" (SN 35.65:3.6).
- **The wager and its limits:** ethics holds even with no afterlife (MN 60:12.5; SN 42.13:23.4–23.5). He rejects an abyss under the ocean (SN 36.4:1.1–1.3), and the views that all experience comes from past deeds (SN 36.21:2.5–2.7) or from a creator (AN 3.61:4.2). Jātaka verses spoken by the nāga hero turn the last into an argument from evil: "Why does not Brahma set his creatures right?" (Ja 543:L275).

Later layers harden kamma. Of the massacre of his own kin, the Dhammapada stories have him say "What they received, however, was entirely just" (DhA 04-03:P170), and Nanda's death was unavoidable wherever he went (DhA 03-08:P16), close to the determinism SN 36.21 rejects. The Paṭṭhāna builds rebirth-linking into its causal system (Paṭṭhāna 1.2:L13). Doubt survives inside the canon: King Milinda says of hell's heat, "I do not believe this assertion" (Mil 3.4.6:1.3).

### 10.2 Miracles [Strong]

*Evidence: 557 texts in 34 collections, 162 of them early; 627 lines support, 52 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 129, early Khuddaka 33, Jātaka 114, late Khuddaka 127, Vinaya 27, Abhidhamma 40, Dhammapada stories 87.*

In DN 11 he is "horrified, repelled, and disgusted" by displays of psychic power and mind-reading. His reason is empirical: a sceptic will put them down to a charm (DN 11:5.1–5.7, 7.8). In the Vinaya he reads Uruvelā Kassapa's mind and flies to Uttarakuru for alms, one of a series of wonders used to win Kassapa over (Kd 1:19.2.1–19.4.4). He uses psychic power on Aṅgulimāla (MN 86:5.2), and DN 16 adds earthquakes and an aeon of life (Book 10). **The texts disagree.**

The whole canon gives the disagreement a shape: the powers are real and claimed, display for persuasion or gain is restrained, and the later the layer, the freer the display. "Whenever I want, I wield the many kinds of psychic power" (SN 16.9:10.1). Yet liberation by wisdom needs no powers (SN 12.70:26.1), wonders fail to convert Kassapa (Thag 6.1:1.1–1.4), and he rebukes a display "for the sake of a miserable wooden bowl" (Kd 15:8.2.17) and refuses magical alms in a famine (Bu-Vb-Pj1:2.2.12), though he also asks Sāgata for "more wonders" (Kd 5:1.7.4). The Jātaka and the Dhammapada stories have him shock drunken women with a ray from his eyebrow (Ja 512:L4). Two late currents run the other way: the Abhidhamma treats the divine eye as an ordinary perceptual channel, open even to worldlings (Paṭṭhāna 1.4:L385), and Nāgasena says Buddhas avoid living by miracles lest people find fault (Mil 5.5.7:L12).

### 10.3 Omniscience [Strong]

*Evidence: 399 texts in 30 collections, 134 of them early; 438 lines support, 51 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 102, early Khuddaka 32, Jātaka 77, late Khuddaka 85, Vinaya 20, Abhidhamma 15, Dhammapada stories 68.*

Modest (MN 71:5.5; MN 90:8.5) against expansive (AN 4.24:2.1). Later layers inflate further. The Buddhavaṃsa's past Buddha is fifty-eight cubits tall and lives ninety thousand years (Bv 8:24.1–27.2). DN 14 makes the Buddha's life a template that every Buddha repeats.

Both poles are early, sometimes in one text. DN 29 says all that is known "has been understood by the Realized One" (DN 29:29.1), but limits his knowledge of the future to knowing he will not be reborn (DN 29:27.4–27.7). He calls himself "the knower of all" (MN 26:25.6), yet says "What I have explained is a tiny amount" (SN 56.31:1.8): vast, not total. He learns of his old teacher's death from a deity (MN 26:22.8–22.10), asks why the Saṅgha has shrunk (SN 54.9:4.2), and settles Dabba's case by formal questioning (Kd 14:4.9.3). Later layers inflate and then patch: "When Buddhas know what is going on, sometimes they ask and sometimes not." (Kd 1:31.4.6–31.4.8); Nāgasena makes omniscience a matter of turning the mind (Mil 5.1.2:1.2); the Dhammapada stories concede a limit "even with the knowledge of omniscience" (DhA 18-09:P14).

### 10.4 Self-reliance against authority [Moderate]

*Evidence: 86 texts in 24 collections, 41 of them early; 82 lines support, 15 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 27, early Khuddaka 14, Jātaka 9, late Khuddaka 15, Vinaya 4, Abhidhamma 12, Dhammapada stories 5.*

"Live as your own island... let the teaching be your island" (DN 16:2.26.1; Book 10 notes the second half is usually dropped). "The teaching and training... shall be your Teacher" (DN 16:6.1.5). Set this against a critic consigned to hell (MN 12:21.4) and rivals called blind (Ud 6.4:16.1).

The island teaching recurs (DN 26:1.7; SN 22.43:1.2), and no one frees another: "I am not able to release anyone in the world who is indecisive, Dhotaka." (Snp 5.6:4.1–4.4). Even he reveres the teaching rather than a person (AN 4.21:7.1–7.2). Against this, the monks say "Our teachings are rooted in the Buddha" (AN 8.83:1.3), MN 38's self-check takes the form of assent to his statements (MN 38:18.1–18.2), and later texts make doubt in the Teacher a fetter (Vb 17:L180) and punish insult to relic worship with a ghostly rebirth (Pv 35:L10).

### 10.5 Inclusion against exclusion [Strong; mixed]

*Evidence: 171 texts in 32 collections, 74 of them early; 113 lines support, 76 complicate, 11 run against. By group: Nikāyas 59, early Khuddaka 15, Jātaka 14, late Khuddaka 32, Vinaya 24, Abhidhamma 15, Dhammapada stories 12.*

Aṅgulimāla, slaves, and women are welcomed as individuals, then restricted as categories (sections 6.5, 8.4). The whole canon confirms the pattern. Individuals: food-carriers and hunters are taught with care (AN 5.99:2.7), and a destitute leper is picked out of a crowd (Ud 5.3:4.5). Categories:
- **Body:** thirty-two conditions, among them blindness, deafness, and old age, bar the going forth (Kd 1:71.2.1–71.2.32); paṇḍakas and intersex people are barred and expelled (Kd 1:61.1.19; Kd 1:68.1.4); disability is a karmic result (MN 129:25.4).
- **Laity and region:** no layperson ends suffering without leaving home (MN 71:11.3); birth "among uneducated foreigners" is a lost chance (SN 56.62:2.1).
- **Other religions:** "Other sects are empty of ascetics." (MN 11:2.1–2.2; AN 4.241:1.2–1.3). Yet he tells a new convert to keep feeding the Jains (AN 8.12:27.1).

Later texts harden the categories: a lay arahant must ordain or die that day (Mil 6.2.2:L6), and eunuchs and children under seven cannot attain insight (Mil 6.3.8:L8).

### 10.6 Tenderness against detachment in grief [Strong; mixed]

*Evidence: 126 texts in 28 collections, 50 of them early; 105 lines support, 42 complicate, 3 run against. By group: Nikāyas 26, early Khuddaka 24, Jātaka 31, late Khuddaka 16, Vinaya 7, Abhidhamma 4, Dhammapada stories 18.*

Section 5.5 gave the pair. The whole canon shows the detachment pole is the stronger, even in the early texts. To a father wailing for his only child: "For our loved ones are a source of sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress." (MN 87:3.8–3.9). To a grieving grandmother: "have no loved ones in the world at all" (Ud 8.8:8.3–8.4). After his two chief disciples die, he says a Realized One "does not sorrow or lament" (SN 47.14:2.9–2.10). The tender pole is thinner: a mother's cry "Let me die, may my child not die!" (AN 3.62:9.1–9.2). The Vinaya gives his father's "Affection for a child cuts deep." after Rāhula is ordained without the family's leave (Kd 1:54.2.8; Kd 1:54.5.2). All three counter-examples are Jātaka renunciations: the Bodhisatta says his young wives will find another to console them (Ja 525:L10), bribes a nurse to take away his clinging son (Ja 525:L40), and calls his motherless son "this accursed boy" (Ja 529:L79). The texts voice the cost: Jāli asks Vessantara "is thy heart a piece of stone" (Ja 547:L587).

### 10.7 The middle way against ascetic ideals [Strong; mixed]

*Evidence: 151 texts in 27 collections, 69 of them early; 106 lines support, 67 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 54, early Khuddaka 15, Jātaka 22, late Khuddaka 26, Vinaya 21, Abhidhamma 8, Dhammapada stories 5.*

Rag robes, tree roots, and fermented urine as lifelong supports (Kd 1:77). The ascetic ideal of Dhp 393–395, which Book 11 found "sits in tension with the middle way".

The rejection is plain: "Thank goodness I’m freed from that pointless grueling work." (SN 4.1:1.5); a painful practice is "inferior because it’s painful" (AN 4.166:3.1). But he will not condemn all austerity (DN 8:3.6), approves Mahākassapa's (SN 16.5:7.2–7.3), and urges the vow "Let the flesh and blood waste away in my body!" (MN 70:27.6). His praise of meditation on ugliness precedes the monks' suicides (SN 54.9:1.3; Bu-Vb-Pj3:1.1.9–1.1.12). Devadatta cites his praise of austerity to demand that it be compulsory (Kd 17:4.1.3), and is refused (Ja 143:L4). Later texts tilt ascetic: the Apadāna recalls the six years of self-torment as the road to Buddhahood (Tha-ap 548:L23), and Nāgasena holds that no one becomes an arahant without keeping ascetic vows in some life (Mil 6.4.2:L19).

### 10.8 Where versions differ [Thin; mixed]

*Evidence: 36 texts in 10 collections, 1 of them early; 26 lines support, 11 complicate, 0 run against. By group: early Khuddaka 1, Jātaka 3, late Khuddaka 14, Vinaya 9, Abhidhamma 6, Dhammapada stories 3.*

The label is low because most differences show only against non-Pali parallels, which the extraction did not read.

- The Kālāma Sutta's Chinese parallel lacks the ten grounds (Book 7).
- The women's impossibilities are absent from MN 115's Madhyama-āgama parallel.
- The "last words" appear three months earlier in DN 16 (Book 10).
- The parallels of DN 31 differ on the wife's and servants' duties (Book 8).
- The wording of SN 22.59 was not fixed (Book 9).
- Two sections of MN 10 are probably later additions (Book 5).
- The chain in MN 118 was joined to its setting in transmission (Book 6).
- The Jātaka frames and the Buddhavaṃsa are late.
- **Added from the evidence:**
  - Aṅgulimāla's ordination alarms people and prompts a ban on ordaining notorious criminals (Kd 1:41.1.1–41.1.2), unlike MN 86.
  - Nāgasena denies that any of his pains came from past kamma (Mil 5.1.8:8.3); the Apadāna traces them to past-life crimes (Tha-ap 392; section 10.9).
  - Anachronisms: the Buddha names the Vibhaṅgas and Khandhakas (Tha-ap 8:L110); Khemā masters the Kathāvatthu (Thi-ap 18:L91); Vinaya and commentary close with Sri Lankan lineages (Pvr 1.1:6.2–6.4; DhA 24-02:P62).
  - The tradition knew: disputes are defined by "“This was spoken by the Buddha”, “This wasn’t spoken by the Buddha”" (Pvr 5:152.6), and rival schools held that a created double taught the Dhamma (Kv 18.2:L4–L5).

### 10.9 The Jātaka Bodhisatta: hero, antagonist, and lapses [Thin; Strong in the Jātaka]

*Evidence: about 90 texts in 7 collections, 9 of them early; about 120 lines, nearly all supporting, none against. By group: Nikāyas 9, Jātaka about 79, late Khuddaka 2. Counted from the new-trait lines plus the Devadatta and past-life identification lines recorded under other traits.*

The root is early but small: in a few discourses he identifies himself with the king, priest, or donor of an old tale (DN 17:2.14.3; DN 19:61.2; AN 9.20:5.4). The Jātaka frame makes this a formula that closes nearly every story, and he usually takes the wise part: "In those days Devadatta was the foolish hawker; and I myself was the wise and good hawker." (Ja 3:L18).

Devadatta is the villain across lives in over sixty Jātaka texts: liar (Ja 113:L4), cruel (Ja 222:L6), would-be murderer: "Devadatta goes about seeking to slay me; he went about with the like intent in bygone days also,—but was unable to slay me." (Ja 21:L4). The apostate Sunakkhatta gets a villain's role too (Ja 545:L112). The edge is hard: Devadatta was swallowed by the earth "because he dealt a blow at my victorious authority" (Ja 457:L4), and two tales present the Devadatta figure's lynching as just (Ja 220:L75; Ja 243:L46). Yet once he casts himself as the low-caste teacher (Ja 474:L33).

The Bodhisatta is not ideal throughout. He abducts a queen (Ja 360:L5), commits adultery with his patron's queen (Ja 431:L7), forgets a friend for forty years (Ja 378:L6), and nearly starves for craving of a mango (Ja 511:L7). MN 81 already has him tell how, as Jotipāla, he called Buddha Kassapa "that shaveling, that fake ascetic" (MN 81:6.8). In the Apadāna he confesses slander and murder in past lives as the root of his present pains: "One time eying wealth I murdered / brothers by a second mother." (Tha-ap 392:L18). The Mahosadha Jātaka praises guile as wisdom: spies under false names, a staged beating, and extortion, all without censure (Ja 542:L187, L198, L267).

### 10.10 The perfections: a vow-bound career of giving [Thin]

*Evidence: about 26 texts in 5 collections, none early; about 38 lines, 35 support, 3 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Jātaka about 11, late Khuddaka about 15 (Cariyāpiṭaka 12).*

The Cariyāpiṭaka and the Jātaka recast the past lives as a career of perfections, each bound by a vow and kept absolutely: "When supplicants come to me it is unbefitting to refuse. Let not my vow be broken" (Cp 3:4.1–4.4); "To give up my own life was as light as a blade of grass." (Cp 12:7.1–7.4). The hare roasts himself for a guest (Cp 10:13.1–13.4), and the Buddha thanks a donor by praising wise men of old who gave their own flesh (Ja 316:L4). The motive is fixed, "solely for awakening" (Cp 8:15.1–15.4), and the self-claims are superlative: "There is no one to equal my resolve" (Cp 26:19.1–19.4). Even sixteen years of feigned deafness count as a perfection (Cp 26:10.1–10.4).

Vessantara is the climax: "for cease from gifts I never will" (Ja 547:L106); giving away his children, "Dearer than my son a hundredfold, a thousandfold, a hundred thousandfold is omniscience!" (Ja 547:L569). The text voices the objection, "but how give those who his own children are?" (Ja 547:L801), and Nāgasena answers with a Cariyāpiṭaka verse: "not that I loved them less— / But Buddhahood more" (Mil 6.3.1:L35). This sits against the case-by-case flexibility of section 8.3 and the middle way: the perfections reward unconditional vows and count giving away a wife and children as virtue.

### 10.11 Field of merit and object of cult [Moderate]

*Evidence: about 65 texts in 17 collections, about 25 of them early; about 72 lines, about 64 support, 0 complicate, about 8 run against. By group: Nikāyas 5, early Khuddaka about 20, late Khuddaka about 31, Vinaya 1, Abhidhamma 4, Dhammapada stories 4.*

The early roots are modest: a Buddha is one of "two … worthy of a monument" (AN 2.52–63:1.1), and kings divide his relics (DN 16:6.24.4). The Vimānavatthu and Petavatthu, counted here among the early Khuddaka, build a merit economy: "Arahants are like fields, Donors are like farmers, A gift is like a seed," (Pv 1:2.1–2.3); a poor woman's sesame wins heaven (Vv 10:L12); relic offerings bear the same fruit as gifts to the living Buddha (Vv 47:L15). Mostly gods and ghosts say this, but he is made to teach that merit reaches the dead through gifts to his own noble disciples (Pv 5:L11).

The late texts multiply the returns. A stool given to a past Buddha yields rule over the earth (Tha-ap 211:L4); a fan yields the quenching of greed, hate, and delusion (Tha-ap 219:L5); "None but deeds done for the monks / has the slightest bit of value" (Tha-ap 297:L6). A relic is worshipped "as though the Best of Men stood there" (Tha-ap 249:L5). In the Buddhavaṃsa he declares himself without equal (Bv 1:71.1–71.4); Nāgasena's formula is "the god over all gods" (Mil 7.2.1:L6); the Parivāra says Buddhas cannot commit an offence (Pvr 7:9.13–9.14).

Against this stands his human status. Udāyī: "Awakened as a human being" (Thag 15.2:1.1–1.2; AN 6.43:5.1–5.4). Doṇa offers him every category, god and human included, and he declines them all: "I will not be a god, brahmin." … "Remember me, brahmin, as a Buddha." (AN 4.36:2.1–2.8; AN 4.36:4.5). To Vakkali: "Why would you want to see this rotting body? One who sees the teaching sees me." (SN 22.87:3.1–3.2), against an Apadāna nun never sated with gazing at his body (Thi-ap 34:L24). The Kathāvatthu defends his birth at Lumbinī and his diet of rice gruel against docetic schools (Kv 18.1:L4; Kv 18.4:L4). But exaltation is early too: he calls himself "A manifestation of divinity, unequaled" (Thag 16.6:14.1–14.2), and Vaṅgīsa hails him "God of gods" (Thag 21.1:71.1).
