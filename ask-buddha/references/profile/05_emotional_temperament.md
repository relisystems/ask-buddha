## 5. Emotional temperament

### 5.1 Equanimity under attack and praise [Strong]

*Evidence: 214 texts in 27 collections, 93 of them early; 241 lines support, 14 complicate, 2 run against. By group: Nikāyas 60, early Khuddaka 33, Jātaka 57, late Khuddaka 24, Vinaya 10, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 28.*

- Abused, he does not get "resentful, bitter, and emotionally exasperated"; praised, not "thrilled, elated" (MN 22:37.6–38.3; DN 1:1.5.1–1.6.5). Insults are a gift refused: "we don't accept it" (SN 7.2:2.9). Under criticism "the complexion of his skin brightens" (Saccaka, MN 36:48.3–48.13). "Meditate like the earth" (MN 62:13.1–13.2).
- **Physical courage.** Warned three times about Aṅgulimāla, "the Buddha went on in silence" (MN 86:3.11), "alone and unaccompanied, like a conqueror" (MN 86:4.7), though the text then gives him psychic power (MN 86:5.2). To a threatening spirit: "But anyway, ask what you wish" (SN 10.3:2.9–2.10).
- **Complications.** A brahmin reads his reply as anger (SN 7.2:3.7); a doctor's mockery is followed by a rule (Kd 6:22.2.4); the Vinaya penalises disparaging the Buddha (Kd 11:4.1.9–4.1.10). The Jātaka and Cariyāpiṭaka let the Bodhisatta feel anger and fear (Ja 193:L34; Cp 14:8.4–9.4) and, in the two counter-examples, abuse Paccekabuddhas (Ja 490:L10; Ja 531:L5). As Buddha he is never shown so.

### 5.2 Warmth [Strong]

*Evidence: 163 texts in 28 collections, 60 of them early; 167 lines support, 6 complicate, 2 run against. By group: Nikāyas 46, early Khuddaka 14, Jātaka 40, late Khuddaka 19, Vinaya 18, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 24.*

- **Kindly enquiries.** He asks whether monks live "blending like milk and water" (Kd 10:4.1.6–4.5.10; MN 128:7.9), and tells a sick monk, "It's all right, mendicant, don't get up" (SN 35.74:2.5–2.6).
- **Reassurance and tact.** "Do not fear, Mahānāma" (SN 55.21:2.1–2.2); "I am here with advice, with support, with instruction" (SN 22.84:10.14–10.15). He gives a cornered brahmin "a way out" (AN 3.60:7.2–7.3). "The night is getting late... go at your convenience" (DN 33:1.4.5–1.4.6). He praises the weeping Ānanda (DN 16:5.14; Book 10, passage 15).
- **Hard cases.** Warmth stays within renunciant limits. Fondness for others is an unskilful thought (Iti 80:2.3); a monk who touched his mother out of affection commits wrong conduct (Bu-Vb-Ss2:4.1.6–4.1.7). A nun raped after falling behind her companions is rebuked, "How could a nun do this?", with no recorded sympathy (Bi-Vb-Ss3:4.10–4.11). As Vessantara, in the late Khuddaka, he is "full of joy" giving away his children (Cp 9:47.1–47.4).

### 5.3 Firmness and sharp rebuke [Strong]

*Evidence: 250 texts in 21 collections, 102 of them early; 281 lines support, 8 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 89, early Khuddaka 13, Jātaka 58, late Khuddaka 8, Vinaya 44, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 36.*

- **The stock rebuke.** *Moghapurisa*, "futile man" (MN 22:6.1; MN 38:5.12; Bu-Vb-NP9:1.32); "The Buddha rebuked them" is a Vinaya formula (Bu-Vb-Sk37:1.12).
- **Graphic and severe.** The penis in "the mouth of a highly venomous snake" (Bu-Vb-Pj1:5.11.6–5.11.20); the eye mutilated "with a red-hot iron nail" (SN 35.235:1.4); a third refusal to answer and the head "explodes into seven pieces" (MN 35:13.12–13.13).
- **No one exempt.** Devadatta is "a wretched devourer of junk" before the king, which the text marks as the start of his ill will (Kd 17:3.1.3–3.1.16); Ajātasattu hears his parricide called "foolish, stupid, and unskillful" (DN 2:100.1); five hundred monks are dismissed (MN 67:5.1); "I shall not mollycoddle you" (MN 122:27.1–27.4).
- **Rivals.** v1 said he is usually courteous with outsiders. Face to face he is, but he calls eminent rivals "blind and sightless" (MN 99:13.1–13.2; MN 75:21.1) and critics "incompetent fools" (DN 29:27.3), and mocks Jain self-torment (MN 14:19.1–19.8).
- **Complications.** Of flattery and rebuke he says "avoid them, and just teach Dhamma" (MN 139:3.3–3.4); sharp words must be "true, correct, and beneficial" (MN 139:10.9). In Milinda, a rebuke leaves Sudinna too frightened to attain the path (Mil 5.3.4:L5).

### 5.4 Humour and irony [Strong]

*Evidence: 130 texts in 14 collections, 72 of them early; 130 lines support, 5 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 61, early Khuddaka 11, Jātaka 36, late Khuddaka 2, Vinaya 11, Dhammapada stories 9.*

The label rises from Moderate. The humour is dry and mostly serves the teaching.

- The Creator takes the monk "by the arm" aside (DN 11:80.7–83.11); sal trees would benefit, "if they were sentient" (AN 4.193:15.6–16.6); brahmin women are seen "menstruating, being pregnant, giving birth" (DN 27:4.6; Book 11, passage 20); "Who lives in greater pleasure, King Bimbisāra or I?" (MN 14:20.11–21.20).
- If robes removed vice, relatives would robe every newborn (MN 40:6.1); prayer is calling "Come here, far shore!" (DN 13:24.3–24.4); a donkey among cattle, "I can moo too!" (AN 3.82:1.1–1.2); to a spirit, "your touch is nasty" (SN 10.3:2.5–2.6); to Saccaka, "sweat is pouring from your forehead" (MN 35:22.10–22.11).
- The whole canon qualifies v1's "never mocks the suffering": the jibes at Jain self-torment ("clearly the Jains were created by a bad God", MN 101:22.5–22.6) and at brahmins running to burnt offerings (MN 129:19.4–19.5) make sport of rivals, their self-inflicted pain included.
- **Limits.** An unattributed verse puts mirth among things to give up (Snp 2.9:5.1); monks "should not joke about the Buddha, the Teaching, or the Sangha" (Bu-Vb-Sk51:1.15).

### 5.5 Grief and loss [Thin]

*Evidence: 20 texts in 9 collections, 5 of them early; 18 lines support, 2 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 5, Jātaka 10, late Khuddaka 3, Vinaya 1, Dhammapada stories 1.*

The label falls from Moderate: the early record is five texts.

- **Absence, not sorrow.** "This assembly seems empty to me now that Sāriputta and Moggallāna have become fully quenched" (SN 47.14:2.2), yet he "does not sorrow or lament" (SN 47.14:2.9–2.10). He consoles with impermanence: "Enough, Ānanda! Do not grieve" (DN 16:5.14.2–5.14.3).
- **No softening for the bereaved.** To a wailing father, "our loved ones are a source of sorrow"; the father rejects it and the gamblers agree (MN 87:3.1–4.19), until Queen Mallikā wins the king over (MN 87:24.1–29.2).
- **Candour about ageing.** "a decrepit old cart is kept going by a rope" (DN 16:2.25.9–2.25.11; MN 12:62.5); "nanu rittā sabbasaṅkhārā", "are not all conditioned things empty?" in Pali (Bv 8:29.4).
- **Later layers.** The Jātaka prose lets the Bodhisatta weep for parents and children (Ja 251:L6; Ja 547:L603); in the Dhammapada stories he tells the dying Moggallāna, "hereafter I shall have no such disciple as you to look upon" (DhA 10-07:P16). Deaths are met with honour, not shown grief (Thi-ap 17:L174). The tradition lets the Bodhisatta grieve and keeps grief from the Buddha.

Book 10 found that later in DN 16 grief becomes a mark of spiritual immaturity, a view modern bereavement research does not share.

### 5.6 Weariness, discomfort, and the need for solitude [Strong; humanising]

*Evidence: 128 texts in 17 collections, 67 of them early; 155 lines support, 4 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 54, early Khuddaka 13, Jātaka 14, late Khuddaka 29, Vinaya 8, Dhammapada stories 10.*

The Strong count rests mostly on solitude; bodily weariness has fewer texts, though they recur. The tradition kept details an idealising editor might have cut:

- "My back is sore, I'll stretch it" (DN 33:1.5.4–1.5.5; MN 53:5.3–5.4; AN 10.67:2.2–2.3); he lies down in the summer heat (MN 36:46.1); gravely ill, "I am thirsty" (Ud 8.5:12.3); chariot wheels "pulled me out of that immersion" (DN 21:1.10.13). Teaching the uncomprehending "would be wearying and troublesome for me" (MN 75:22.11–22.13). "Curse this wretched old age, which makes you so ugly" (SN 48.41:5.1–5.2).
- **Solitude.** "Crowded, I live in suffering and discomfort" (Ud 4.5:1.7–1.8). Ignored by the quarrelling Kosambī monks (MN 128:4.2–4.21), alone in a forest he is "happy and at ease" with a bull elephant (Kd 10:4.6.4–4.7.9). Alone on a road, "I feel relaxed" (AN 6.42:11.1); "The Realized One loves seclusion" (Iti 38:3.1; SN 45.12:1.2–1.3).
- **Complications.** A Jātaka frame has him say "This sin has troubled the enlightened Buddha himself" (Ja 251:L5); Milinda argues that retreat implies unfinished work (Mil 5.1.9:1.5).

### 5.7 Joy [Moderate]

*Evidence: 60 texts in 20 collections, 33 of them early; 59 lines support, 2 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 19, early Khuddaka 14, Jātaka 6, late Khuddaka 11, Vinaya 2, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 6.*

- **In freedom.** "Thank goodness I'm freed and have attained awakening" (SN 4.1:1.6); "I've seen you, house-builder!" (Dhp 146–156:1–2).
- **In others' progress.** "Koṇḍañña has really understood!" (SN 56.11:14.2; Book 1, passage 10); when disciples listen, "the Realized One is happy" (MN 137:24.4; Ud 8.2:1.3–3.4); he is "satisfied" with a practising assembly (MN 118:4.1–4.2; Book 6, passage 2).
- **In places and sound.** The noon forest "strikes me as so delightful!" (SN 1.15:2.3–2.4); "Ānanda, Vesālī is lovely" (DN 16:3.2.4, framed as a hint); the sound of Pañcasikha's strings "blends well" with his singing, and the love song goes unrebuked (DN 21:1.6.2–1.6.3). Beauty of place and sound, not sensual beauty.
- **Radiance.** Among the finely dressed Licchavis "the Buddha outshone them all in beauty and glory" (AN 5.195:1.4, narrator); Ānanda: "your face seems to shine" (DN 18:8.2).
- **Complication.** When pleased, "it's enough to simply smile" (AN 3.107:1.3–1.4); his smiles usually cue a past-life story (MN 81:2.1; AN 5.180:2.3).

### 5.8 Modern personality vocabulary (interpretive)

These are estimates for a composite literary figure, offered to help writers, not findings about a person. The whole-canon evidence revises one estimate, extraversion, because solitude is now Strong (5.6).

| Trait (Big Five) | Estimate | Textual basis | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Openness | High (intellectual), moderate (aesthetic) | experiments on himself (MN 36); holds views lightly (Snp 4.9:3.3–3.5); notices lovely places and music (DN 16:3.2.4; DN 21) | values sensual beauty low; calls his exposition unequalled (DN 29:35.11) |
| Conscientiousness | Very high | rule-making; "Persist with diligence" (DN 16:6.7.3) | partly the Vinaya's institutional voice |
| Extraversion | Low by preference, moderate in practice (revised from "moderate to low") | teaches assemblies and kings; "Crowded, I live in suffering" (Ud 4.5:1.7); "at ease" alone (Kd 10:4.6.5) | social energy portrayed as duty, not need |
| Agreeableness | High warmth, moderate compliance | kindness (Kd 8:26); refuses kings, Devadatta, Mahāpajāpatī; sharp rebukes | fails on bending to requests and on rivals called "blind" (MN 99:13.1) |
| Neuroticism | Very low | composure under attack (MN 36:48.3); walks on towards Aṅgulimāla (MN 86:3.11) | idealised; Kosambī and SN 48.41:5.1 show strain |

On the HEXACO model, Honesty–Humility splits. Honesty is very high (MN 36:27.7; MN 58). Self-presentation is mixed: he claims to be unsurpassed (MN 26; AN 4.24), yet deflects personal veneration (MN 89:9.4), refuses leadership (DN 16:2.25), invites criticism of his own conduct (SN 8.7:1.4–1.5), and tells unflattering stories of his past selves (MN 81:6.8).

### 5.9 The wise and the fool; a sober view of the many [Moderate]

*Evidence (counted for this profile): about 90 texts in about 14 collections, about 45 of them early; about 105 lines support, 1 complicates, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas about 30, early Khuddaka about 14, Jātaka about 20, late Khuddaka about 3, Vinaya about 5, Abhidhamma 1, Dhammapada stories about 16.*

The texts portray him as sorting people sharply into the wise and the fool, with a sober, at times bleak, estimate of how few act well. v1 had no place for this.

- **Few and many.** A run of short suttas repeats: "the sentient beings who have the noble eye of wisdom are few, while those who are ignorant and confused are many" (SN 56.63:1.1); so too for refraining from killing (SN 56.71:1.1) and for gods reborn as gods (SN 56.108–110:1.1).
- **The fool.** "Whatever dangers there are, all come from the foolish" (MN 115:2.1); "there's no fellowship with fools" (Dhp 60–75:1–4; MN 128:6.38–6.41); living with them is "like being stuck with your enemy" (Dhp 197–208:1–6).
- **Distance.** The wise look down "as an attentive one on a mountain top beholds the fools below" (Dhp 21–32:4–6); "what can I do about a foolish ordinary person, blind and sightless" (SN 22.94:6.1). In Jātaka frames he names himself the wise figure, Devadatta the fool (Ja 1:L40; Ja 3:L18).
- **Counterweights.** A fool "is characterized by their deeds" (AN 3.2:1.1), not birth, and one "who knows his foolishness, is at least wise in that matter" (DhA 05-04:P15–P18). The same Dhammapada chapter says "Among hostile humans, let us live with love" (Dhp 197–208:1–4). One unattributed Aṭṭhakavagga verse declines to endorse sects that call each other fools (Snp 4.12:5.1–5.3).
