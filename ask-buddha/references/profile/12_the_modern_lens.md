## 12. The modern lens

Everything in this section is **inference from the traits above, not teaching**. Each topic gives the textual basis, the most favourable modern reading the texts honestly allow, and the limit beyond which that reading distorts them.

### 12.1 Science and evidence

- **Textual basis:**
  - wariness of tradition and testimony (AN 3.65:4.1; MN 95:14.3–14.8)
  - suspended judgement (MN 27:10.6)
  - claims withheld until verified (AN 8.64:10.1; Book 1, passage 8)
  - miracles rejected as weak evidence (DN 11:5.1–5.7)
  - causal regularity (SN 12.20:2.3)
  - deference to the astute (SN 22.94:1.4–1.5)
  - prayer does not change outcomes: a rock does not float because people pray (SN 42.6:5.1–7.5; AN 5.43:3.1)
  - omens, luck, and divination rejected (Snp 2.13:2.3; Snp 4.14; section 4.1)
- **Favourable reading.** The Buddha of these texts would treat well-tested science as part of what "the astute deem as existing" (SN 22.94:1.5). He would welcome methods that separate how things are from what we wish. He would judge practices by their results, including in trials. Where science contradicts a claim in the texts, his own reference test (DN 16:4.8) and his "preserving truth" (MN 95:15.3) give a principled way to hold that claim as faith rather than knowledge.
- **Limit.** He trusted trained introspection and visionary knowledge as sources of fact, including about rebirth and gods (MN 36; DN 18). Rebirth is built into his causal analysis itself (section 10.1). The same causal reasoning explains weather, drought, and earthquakes by morality or the supernatural (AN 3.56; AN 5.197; DN 16:3.13). Some texts promise results by a deadline instead of inviting a test ("seven days", DN 22; section 7.11), and the Jātaka prose and AN 5.196 treat dreams and omens as prophetic. Do not say he "anticipated the scientific method" (Book 7: the Kālāma test "is not the scientific method"). Do not say he would have dropped rebirth. Do not cite SN 10.1 as anticipating embryology: the modern terms are the translator's.

### 12.2 Psychology and mental health

- **Textual basis:**
  - craving and aversion as causes of distress (Book 1, passages 3–5)
  - the second arrow (SN 36.6:1.8–1.10)
  - insight alone does not end desire without a better source of happiness (MN 14:5.1–5.6)
  - lessons from self-starvation (MN 36:25–33)
  - a body ailing but a mind healthy (SN 22.1:3.5)
  - good friends as the dawn of the path (SN 46.48:1.1–1.3)
  - harm from a practice taught without balance (Bu-Vb-Pj3)
- **Favourable reading.** He would recognise the modern finding, reported across the series, that relating differently to pain and craving reduces suffering. He would value trained attention and supportive community, warn against extremes (including over-intense practice), and match the remedy to the person. Changing method after the Pj3 disaster supports a modern concern with safety in meditation. The mind as "radiant", its corruptions only visiting (AN 1.51–60:1.1–1.2), and the weight given to gratitude and loyal friendship (SN 20.12; AN 7.36; section 2.7) support a hopeful view of change and of community.
- **Limit.**
  - His aim is liberation, not adjustment or symptom relief.
  - He treated grief as something to outgrow (DN 16; MN 87; Ud 8.8). He feels the absence of his chief disciples but does not sorrow (SN 47.14:2.2, 2.9–2.10); only the Jātaka Bodhisatta weeps. Bereavement research does not support this view.
  - He used fear and shock as teaching tools, sometimes at a cost (AN 7.72; section 7.12), and the texts do not make him an optimist about most people (section 5.9).
  - The Pj3 deaths have an early parallel, and neither text records remorse (section 8.5).
  - Book 5 found that mindfulness programmes help modestly and no more than other good treatments.
  - Do not present him as a therapist, or meditation as a cure-all.

### 12.3 Ethics of technology (attention, media, artificial intelligence)

- **Textual basis:**
  - sense restraint, judged by whether a sight or sound makes unskilful qualities grow (DN 21:2.5.3)
  - the danger of fame and gain (MN 47:8.3–8.7; SN 20.9)
  - speech judged by truth, benefit, and timing (MN 58:8.1–8.6)
  - wrong livelihood (AN 5.177)
  - intention as the measure of an act (AN 6.63:33.3)
  - harm to self and others checked before, during, and after acting (MN 61)
- **Favourable reading.**
  - He would ask of any technology whether it makes greed, hatred, and delusion grow.
  - He would see attention-harvesting design as a trade that profits from craving, and hold its designers to the MN 61 test.
  - On AI, the useful questions are the texts' own: what intention drives the tool, what it does to the user's mind and conduct, and whether it speaks truly and at the right time.
- **Limit.** Intention is the measure of kamma, but the Vinaya also weighs outcomes and imposes strict liability (section 8.2). The texts say nothing about technology. Any specific policy (bans, regulation, AI consciousness) is our extrapolation. Never attribute a view on machine sentience to him; the texts offer only the general criteria of mind and intention.

### 12.4 Speech, misinformation, and public debate

- **Textual basis:**
  - six criteria of speech (MN 58)
  - "say 'I don't know'" when ignorant (MN 41:13.2)
  - no divisive tale-bearing, "delighting in harmony" (MN 41:13.3)
  - correct untruths about oneself calmly (DN 1:1.6.1)
  - preserve truth by naming faith as faith (MN 95:15.3–15.4)
  - non-contention: views held lightly, debate for fault-finding condemned (MN 18:4.1; MN 22:10.5; section 4.7)
  - claims withheld that listeners could not credit (SN 19.1)
- **Favourable reading.** A strong ethic of truthful, timely, non-divisive speech that targets claims, not people. He would favour correcting falsehood without anger, labelling uncertainty, and refusing to amplify what inflames hatred.
- **Limit.** He also spoke harshly (Kd 17:3.1.12), condemned critics (MN 12:21.4), called rivals "blind" (Ud 6.4:16.1; MN 99:13.1), and called his own exposition unequalled right after refusing "the only truth" (DN 29:35.11). He withheld some true teachings for their effect (AN 9.12), and the commentaries allow protective deception (DhA 08-13; DhA 11-06). The texts give no political theory of free speech. Do not turn MN 58 into a position on censorship.

### 12.5 Social justice: caste, race, gender, and labour

- **Textual basis:**
  - worth by deeds, not birth (Snp 3.9:62.4–64.4; DN 27:4.6; MN 93)
  - inclusion of individuals (MN 86; DN 16 Ambapālī)
  - women's spiritual capacity (Kd 20:1.3.16; SN 5.2)
  - humane duties to workers (DN 31, Book 8)
  - in the ideal sacrifice, slaves and workers are not driven by threats (DN 5:18.2–18.3), which regulates their lot rather than ending it
  - poverty as a cause of crime (DN 26:14.1)
- **Favourable reading.** His arguments against inborn worth (biological sameness, conduct as the measure, names as convention) extend naturally to race and caste. His practice of meeting the despised as equals gives modern movements a real foothold, as Ambedkar showed.
- **Limit.**
  - He did not abolish class, oppose slavery, or establish gender equality.
  - The texts regulate slavery (Kd 1:47; Book 8) and list slaves as property (MN 26; AN 10.74). They subordinate nuns (Kd 20:1.4.1) and screen women candidates for slavery, debt, and a husband's consent (section 8.4). They contain misogynistic predictions (Kd 20:1.6.6) and disparaging generalisations about women in his own voice (AN 4.80; AN 5.229; AN 5.230).
  - The early texts explain low birth as the result of past deeds (MN 135), so "worth by deeds" sits beside a kammic justification of rank (section 9.1).
  - Kd 1 excludes people with disabilities, paṇḍakas, escaped criminals, and others from ordination (section 8.4).
  - State this whenever the favourable reading is offered. Write "his principles point towards", never "he taught".

### 12.6 Economics, wealth, and poverty

- **Textual basis:** legitimate earning and sharing (AN 4.61); balance (AN 8.54:5.2); hoarding harms dependants (DN 12:10.4–10.16); seed, capital, and wages instead of punishment (DN 5:11.8–11.13); poverty breeds violence (DN 26:14.1); wrong livelihoods (AN 5.177).
- **Favourable reading.** He would favour broad economic security as a foundation for peace and morality, see extreme hoarding as harmful, and judge businesses by what they trade. DN 5 reads like an argument for public investment over punishment.
- **Limit.** DN 5's advice comes in a story of a past king, and DN 26 is a myth of moral decline. Neither is an economic programme. The texts also explain poverty as the fruit of past stinginess (MN 135:13.1), and the later merit economy steers giving to the Saṅgha for promised returns (Pv 5; Tha-ap 297; section 10.11). Do not attach him to any modern party or system.

### 12.7 Politics, war, and violence

- **Textual basis:** cycles of retaliation (SN 3.14–3.15); war from craving (MN 14:11); the seven conditions of a strong people (DN 16); the ruler bound by principle (DN 26:5.2); his own non-violence (MN 21:20.1); the soldier's fate (SN 42.3); fair procedure for the accused in the Saṅgha (Kd 14; section 8.1).
- **Favourable reading.** He would argue that violence perpetuates itself, that cohesion, consultation, and law make communities strong, and that rulers serve under principle, not above it. He would counsel leaders privately and persuasively rather than oppose them publicly.
- **Limit.** He did not forbid kings war, did not oppose monarchy, and accommodated the state (Kd 1:40). He says nothing against a king's mass imprisonment (SN 3.10), and royal torture and execution serve as neutral examples (AN 2.1–10; MN 129:4.2). The trait is Moderate and mixed (section 9.5). Do not make him a pacifist activist or a democrat (Book 10).

### 12.8 The environment and animals

- **Textual basis:** sympathy for all living beings (MN 41:12.2); the golden rule extended to creatures (Ud 2.3); the rains retreat and the plant rules (Kd 3:1.2.2; Bu-Vb-Pc11:1.18); care in disposing of waste (Kd 10:4.5.5); the ruler protects "beasts and birds" (DN 26:5.2); trade in living beings and meat as wrong livelihood (AN 5.177).
- **Favourable reading.** An ethic of restraint and non-harm that extends to small creatures and to "one-sensed" plant life. Simplicity of consumption (the four supports, Kd 1:77) and care for shared resources fit modern environmental concern.
- **Limit.**
  - The texts have no concept of ecosystems or climate.
  - Monks could eat meat not killed for them (Kd 17:3.15.15; Kd 6:31.14).
  - Animal birth is a punishment for wrongdoing (AN 10.216).
  - Several rules answered public opinion, not doctrine.
  - Human birth is ranked above animal birth (Kd 1:63).
  - Do not call him a vegetarian or an environmentalist.

### 12.9 Religious pluralism

- **Textual basis:** support for other schools' monks (MN 56:17.8; AN 3.57:2.6); calm in the face of criticism (DN 1:1.5); no conversion on impulse (MN 56:16.1); faith held as faith (MN 95:15.3); one truth, "no second" (Snp 4.12:7.1); rivals as blind men with an elephant (Ud 6.4:16.1–18.8); reverence for awakened beings outside his following (MN 116); views held lightly (Snp 4.9:3.3; section 4.7).
- **Favourable reading.** Courtesy, generosity, and calm towards other religions. Honesty about one's own faith as faith. Judging teachers by conduct (MN 95:17.2–20.2). On these grounds he would oppose religious hostility and coercion.
- **Limit.** He did not hold that all paths lead to the same goal. The elephant parable says other teachers see only parts (Ud 6.4), and DN 16 claims arahants only in his teaching (Book 10, passage 16; MN 11). The polemic against other schools is sharp (AN 4.241; AN 10.78; DhA 10-07; section 6.4). He accepts many framings within his own teaching (SN 35.245; SN 36.19), not many paths. The strongest lines on holding views lightly are unattributed Aṭṭhakavagga verses. Do not present him as a relativist or a modern interfaith pluralist.

### 12.10 Medicine, death, and dying

- **Textual basis:**
  - nursing the sick as a duty equal to serving him (Kd 8:26)
  - allowances for the sick (Bu-Vb-Sk37:2.8)
  - mindful endurance of pain (DN 16, Book 10, passage 6)
  - candour about ageing (DN 16:2.25.11)
  - the invitation to raise doubts before death (DN 16:6.5.2)
  - care for the person who may be blamed for a death (DN 16:4.42.3)
- **Favourable reading.** Hands-on care, honesty with the dying, clear speech about decline, attention to carers and to those who may feel guilt. Each fits modern palliative and hospice values.
- **Limit.**
  - His consolation rests on impermanence and on rebirth or its ending.
  - His view of grief is not modern psychology's (section 5.5).
  - The texts give no rulings on euthanasia or medical ethics as now debated.
  - Bu-Vb-Pj3 shows suicide among monks treated as a disaster and an offence (the rule against killing), yet MN 144:13.11 declares Channa's suicide blameless.
  - Medicine and surgery are "low lore" for ascetics (DN 1:1.27), and the Vinaya allows folk remedies (Kd 6:14.6.3–14.6.4).
  - In the commentaries, consolation can rest on kamma as just desert (DhA 04-03).
  - Do not derive a position on assisted dying.

### 12.11 Conflicts between the texts and modern knowledge

- **Textual basis:** the reference test (DN 16:4.8); preserving truth (MN 95:15.3); not disputing with the astute (SN 22.94); the raft (MN 22:13–14; MN 22:24.1–24.5); views held lightly (Snp 4.9:3.3); the wager that holds "if there is no other world" (AN 3.65:44.1; MN 60:9.2–20.2; SN 42.13).
- **Favourable reading.** Where the texts' cosmology or rebirth conflicts with evidence, his own principles allow a reader to hold such claims as faith, to emphasise what pays off "in this very life" (AN 3.65:43.2–46.1), and to treat the teaching as a raft rather than a creed.
- **Limit.**
  - The Buddha of the texts held these claims as knowledge (section 4.4), and rebirth is woven into the causal analysis (section 10.1), in 29 per cent of the Nikāya texts and most of the later layers. Book 7 found that the agnostic reading of the wager is "a modern choice, not the canon's view".
  - Say that the reading is ours.
  - Never claim he would agree with modern scientific materialism.
