## 6. Interpersonal style

### 6.1 With disciples [Strong]

*Evidence: 367 texts in 28 collections, 190 of them early; 405 lines support, 20 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 158, early Khuddaka 32, Jātaka 62, late Khuddaka 31, Vinaya 34, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 48.*

- **Delegation and endorsement.** He hands the floor to Sāriputta and praises him: "Good, good" (DN 33:1.5.3, 3.4.1–3.4.3; Ānanda in MN 53:26.2–26.3). He endorses disciples' expositions as his own (MN 18:21.1–21.2; MN 114:13.1–13.2).
- **Teaching to the person.** A former lute player gets the lute simile (Kd 5:1.15.11–1.16.3).
- **Listening to disciples.** Ānanda's arguments change his mind on the dead of Magadha, "for many people would gain confidence" (DN 18:4.1–7.4), and on nuns (Kd 20:1.3.11–1.3.19).
- **Defending disciples.** He rejects the rumour that his chief disciples have defected: "It's impossible" (Kd 17:4.1.25). In eleven Dhammapada stories (commentary) he publicly clears monks whom their peers accuse of lying about an attainment, calling each "my son" (DhA 25-10:P15; DhA 26-14:P37).
- **Inviting doubt** three times at the end (DN 16:6.5.2–6.6.2). **Firmness** when needed (section 5.3).

**Friction.** He tells Ānanda he would have lived on had Ānanda asked a third time (DN 16:3.40.8). Quarrelling monks tell him to "remain passive" (MN 128:4.4–4.6). In the commentaries he is accused of favouritism (DhA 01-08:P74), and he draws his reluctant brother Nanda into the order by leaving his bowl in Nanda's hands (DhA 01-09:P14). Reverence stops Nanda saying "I do not wish to go forth" (DhA 01-09:P15); nymphs are then used "as a lure" (DhA 01-09:P52). The commentary admires this; the consent is shaped by deference.

### 6.2 With kings [Moderate]

*Evidence: 110 texts in 17 collections, 28 of them early; 118 lines support, 4 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 26, early Khuddaka 2, Jātaka 44, late Khuddaka 6, Vinaya 14, Dhammapada stories 18.*

- **Courteous, never servile, and frank.** He questions Pasenadi's kisses on his feet (MN 89:9.4) and tells him "old age and death are advancing upon you" (SN 3.25:4.1).
- **Counsel on war without condemning the king** (SN 3.14:5.1–5.4; SN 3.15:7.1–9.6), and economic advice through a story: seed, capital, and wages instead of punishment (DN 5:11.1–11.13).
- **Counsellor in the commentaries.** Most royal episodes are here: he urges just rule (Ja 521:L4) and tells Pasenadi to keep Mallikā (Ja 306:L6). In Ja 465 the narrator has him resolve, "I must help my kindred" (L29), a loyalty to his clan found only in this frame.
- **Accommodation to royal power.** When the judges propose beheading the preceptor of ordained soldiers, he bars the king's men from going forth (Kd 1:40.3.1–40.3.9). He rules: "You should comply with the wishes of kings" (Kd 3:4.3.4).

### 6.3 With brahmins [Strong]

*Evidence: 102 texts in 18 collections, 64 of them early; 109 lines support, 7 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 49, early Khuddaka 15, Jātaka 11, late Khuddaka 11, Vinaya 5, Dhammapada stories 11.*

- **He works from their premises** (DN 12:10.1–13.9; MN 107:2.1–3.3) and redefines "brahmin" by conduct (Snp 3.9:60.1–64.4; Dhp 393–395, Book 11).
- **He can be cutting:** brahmin principles survive "among hounds, but not among brahmins" (AN 5.191:1.1). See section 5.4 on birth.
- **Yet he wins them over** (SN 7.1:8.3–8.4; MN 100:42.2–42.3), often accepting hospitality by silence (DN 12:5.5).
- **Face-saving for the defeated.** Having humbled Ambaṭṭha, he rescues him from his jeering companions: "Why don't I get him out of this?" (DN 3:1.23.2–1.23.4). He gives the anxious Soṇadaṇḍa a question he can answer well (DN 4:11.2–11.3).

Against this, the student Uttara is left "silent, dismayed" (MN 152:2.11). In the Jātaka the Bodhisatta calls brahmins "faithless and ingrate alway" (Ja 544:L65).

### 6.4 With ascetics of other schools [Strong]

*Evidence: 177 texts in 28 collections, 95 of them early; 167 lines support, 42 complicate, 3 run against. By group: Nikāyas 77, early Khuddaka 18, Jātaka 16, late Khuddaka 32, Vinaya 10, Abhidhamma 7, Dhammapada stories 17.*

- **He seeks rivals out**, visiting their communities (DN 9:2.2–2.3; MN 77:3.3–3.4) and checking their views in person (MN 101:3.1–3.2). He starts from common ground (DN 8:5.2–5.3) but debates hard (MN 14:15.1–21.21; MN 36).
- **The test is understanding, not sect.** Outsiders who grasp the truths are "true ascetics and brahmins" (SN 56.22:2.1–2.2). He honours the "independent Buddhas", awakened outside his following (MN 116:3.7–3.8).
- **He is generous to their followers** (MN 56:17.8; AN 3.57:2.6–2.9) and calm under criticism (DN 1:1.5.1–1.6.5).

**The polemic is real** (45 lines complicate or run against). Rivals are "blind and sightless" (Ud 6.4:16.1–18.8); "Other sects are empty of ascetics" (AN 4.241:1.2); Jains have "ten bad qualities" (AN 10.78:1.1). A convert's probation turns on whether he still praises his old teacher (Kd 1:38.7.2–38.7.3). Later layers turn triumphal (Tha-ap 3:L89). In the Dhammapada stories he says naked ascetics burned alive "suffered precisely that form of death which they deserved" (DhA 10-07:P27).

### 6.5 With women [Strong; mixed; hard case]

*Evidence: 171 texts in 20 collections, 55 of them early; 119 lines support, 55 complicate, 26 run against. By group: Nikāyas 44, early Khuddaka 11, Jātaka 37, late Khuddaka 10, Vinaya 38, Dhammapada stories 31.*

"Strong" measures how widely the theme is recorded: 81 of the 200 lines complicate or run against.

**Affirming:**

- He keeps his word to the courtesan Ambapālī over the Licchavi nobles who call her "wench" (DN 16:2.16.3, 2.18.6; Book 10, passage 5).
- Asked whether women can attain every stage, he says "Yes, they would" (Kd 20:1.3.15–1.3.16; Somā, SN 5.2:4.1–5.4).
- He praises named women: "The nun Dhammadinnā is astute, Visākha, she has great wisdom" (MN 44:30.4). Two laywomen are "a standard and a measure" for his female lay disciples (AN 4.176:4.2–4.3). He names sufferings "undergone by ladies and not by men" (SN 37.3:1.1).
- A raped nun is blameless: "There's no offense for one who doesn't consent" (Bu-Vb-Pj1:10.5.8).
- "Some women are better than men" (SN 3.16:1.5–3.2), but the verse praises "a devoted wife" whose son may rule, so it does not state equality.

**Restricting and disparaging:**

- He refuses Mahāpajāpatī three times: "Let it be, Gotami, don't pursue this idea." She later stands at his gate "covered in dust, her feet swollen" (Kd 20:1.1.4–1.2.6).
- He imposes eight principles of deference (Kd 20:1.4.1): "it is forbidden for nuns to criticize monks, but it is not forbidden for monks to criticize nuns" (AN 8.51:18.1; Bi-Vb-Pc95).
- Women's going forth shortens the teaching's life to five hundred years (Kd 20:1.6.4–1.6.6; AN 8.51:26.1–27.1).
- Women cannot be a Buddha, Sakka, Māra, or Brahmā (MN 115:15.1–15.6). Anālayo (2009) found no such list in the Madhyama-āgama parallel.
- **In his own voice, in the Nikāyas:** "This is a lady's venom: usually she's very lustful" (AN 5.230:2.4–2.5). Women's exclusion from councils and work is put down to their faults (AN 4.80:2.6). A laywoman with a fair question is "a foolish incompetent aunty" (AN 6.44:5.1). A bride offered to him is a body "full of piss and shit" (Snp 4.9:1.3–1.4); the commentary records her lasting resentment (DhA 02-01:P181).
- **In the Vinaya:** sex with a woman is worse than a viper's mouth (Bu-Vb-Pj1:5.11.19). Nuns raped while travelling alone receive the stock rebuke, and the rules that follow protect them by confining them (Bi-Vb-Ss3:2.20–2.21; Bi-Vb-Pc37:1.3–1.8). The nun Mettiyā is expelled; the monks who incited her are only called to account (Bu-Vb-Ss8:1.9.13–1.9.20).
- **In the Jātaka prose, a commentary layer:** he closes stories with verdicts such as "womankind are ungrateful and treacherous" (Ja 425:L20; Ja 63:L19). As Bodhisatta he gives away his wife (Cp 9:50.1–50.4) and has his future wife dragged off as a test (Ja 542:L113). Women avoid him because they hear he "hates beauty" (Thi-ap 36:L14).

**Reading:** the texts affirm women's spiritual capacity and enact their institutional subordination. The whole canon sharpens v1: the hostile generalisations are not only late. Several stand in the Nikāyas in his own voice, and they multiply in the Vinaya and the Jātaka. Some are misogynistic by any modern standard. Book 10 concluded that "champion of women's equality" claims more than the texts will bear.

### 6.6 With lay people [Strong]

*Evidence: 211 texts in 26 collections, 102 of them early; 220 lines support, 12 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 85, early Khuddaka 17, Jātaka 39, late Khuddaka 17, Vinaya 31, Abhidhamma 2, Dhammapada stories 20.*

- **Practical advice** on wealth (AN 4.61; AN 8.54), households (DN 31, Book 8), and marriage between equals in faith and ethics (AN 4.55:3.1).
- **Lay people can go far** (SN 45.24:3.1–3.2), and monks must make donors' gifts fruitful (MN 39:2.4).
- **Any greeting is accepted**, even silence (MN 41:3.1; Book 7, passage 1). Lay criticism shapes the rules (section 8.1).

Yet the lay role is often to supply the monks (AN 4.60:2.3), and critics say he is "making us childless" (Kd 1:24.5.3).

### 6.7 With the sick, the dying, and the grieving [Moderate]

*Evidence: 127 texts in 24 collections, 47 of them early; 142 lines support, 12 complicate, 0 run against. By group: Nikāyas 34, early Khuddaka 13, Jātaka 20, late Khuddaka 8, Vinaya 28, Abhidhamma 1, Dhammapada stories 23.*

- He nurses the sick himself (Kd 8:26). He visits the sick: "It's all right, Vakkali, don't get up" (SN 22.87:2.4–2.5), and goes to one monk because he is junior and unknown (SN 35.74:2.1).
- He tells the ailing Nakula's father to train so that "Though my body is ailing, my mind will be healthy" (SN 22.1:2.1–3.5). In his own illness he endures mindfully (DN 16; Book 10, passage 6).
- With the grieving he is tender or blunt (section 5.5; Thag 1.44:1.1–1.4). Kisāgotamī's mustard seed is **commentary**, not canon.

Yet comfort often takes the form of insight practice (AN 5.121:3.2). He bars the sick from ordaining to get treatment (Kd 1:39.7.4), and seems to approve Godhika's suicide (SN 4.23:7.1–7.4).

### 6.8 With wrongdoers [Strong]

*Evidence: 194 texts in 26 collections, 54 of them early; 191 lines support, 36 complicate, 1 runs against. By group: Nikāyas 44, early Khuddaka 10, Jātaka 55, late Khuddaka 12, Vinaya 45, Abhidhamma 4, Dhammapada stories 24.*

- **Aṅgulimāla.** "I've stopped, Aṅgulimāla—now you stop" (MN 86:5.9); tamed "without rod or sword" (MN 86:13.7). Stoned and bleeding, the new monk is told: "Endure it, brahmin!" (MN 86:17.6–17.7).
- **But the institution closed the door** to "a notorious criminal" (Kd 1:41.1.1–41.1.8) and to a remorseful matricide (Kd 1:64.2.8).
- **Confession accepted.** "Since you have recognized your mistake for what it is … I accept it", even from the parricide Ajātasattu and a would-be assassin (DN 2:100.2; Kd 17:3.7.9).
- **Proportion and prevention** (Kd 13:3.1.1–3.3.16; Bu-Vb-Sk37:2.23–2.24; Kd 1:52.1.1–52.1.8).

The hard edge: Devadatta is "irredeemable", bound for hell for an eon (AN 8.7:1.7; Iti 89:2.1); corrupt monks are "trash" (AN 8.10:10.1–10.2). The Jātaka makes Devadatta a villain in life after life (Ja 505:L55).

### 6.9 Slavery [Moderate; mixed; hard case]

*Evidence: 79 texts in 22 collections, 32 of them early; 13 lines support, 73 complicate, 3 run against. By group: Nikāyas 26, early Khuddaka 6, Jātaka 16, late Khuddaka 12, Vinaya 7, Abhidhamma 1, Dhammapada stories 11.*

"Mixed" understates it: 76 of the 89 lines complicate or run against a favourable reading.

- A runaway slave is ordained. After the owners complain, the ruling is: "You shouldn't give the going forth to a slave" (Kd 1:47.1.1–47.1.11).
- DN 31 gives masters humane duties (DN 31:32.1–32.2). Book 8 found that it regulates slavery and never questions it.
- **The Nikāyas take slavery for granted.** Slaves are listed with livestock (MN 26:6.2) and as part of a noble disciple's prosperity (AN 10.74:1.3). A bondservant's wish that his master not prosper is a defilement (AN 10.23:5.4–5.5). Monks refuse gifts of slaves (DN 1:1.10.7–1.10.11): an abstention, not a condemnation.
- **Against this:** trade in living beings is wrong livelihood (AN 5.177:1.3). In the ideal sacrifice no one works "under threat of punishment": "Those who wished to work did so" (DN 5:18.2–18.3). He notes the weeping labourers of royal sacrifices (MN 51:10.8) and uses release from servitude as an image of joy (DN 2:72.1–72.2). Abroad, "masters may become servants" (MN 93:6.5).
- **Later layers** explain slavery by past deeds (Vv 50:L9; Ja 545:L44) and list slaves among the rewards of merit (Tha-ap 430:L24). As Bodhisatta he retrieves a runaway slave (Ja 127:L9); as Vessantara he gives his children as slaves, and Nāgasena defends this by the custom of selling children (Mil 6.3.1:L26). One commentarial scene points the other way: to a brahmin seizing his escaped slave, now an arahant, he says, "His burden has fallen from him, Brahmin" (DhA 26-19:P11).

The texts show kindness within slavery and monastic abstention from it, but never question the institution.

### 6.10 With animals and non-human beings [Strong]

*Evidence: 149 texts in 22 collections, 50 of them early; 153 lines support, 11 complicate, 1 runs against. By group: Nikāyas 32, early Khuddaka 18, Jātaka 43, late Khuddaka 29, Vinaya 14, Abhidhamma 1, Dhammapada stories 12.*

- He greets spirits with a wish for their happiness (DN 21:1.8.9) and teaches gods as he teaches monks (MN 37:3.1–3.2).
- "I love the footless creatures" (AN 4.67:6.1–6.4). The animal's pain is part of the slaughterer's wrong (MN 55:12.4).
- An elephant attends him (Kd 10:4.7.1–4.7.9). A serpent-dragon ordained in disguise is sent away gently and weeps; animals may not be ordained (Kd 1:63.4.1–63.5.5).

Yet in law an animal's life counts for less (Bu-Vb-Pj3:4.5.12–4.5.14), and in the Jātaka the Bodhisatta has a doe poisoned (Ja 93:L6).
